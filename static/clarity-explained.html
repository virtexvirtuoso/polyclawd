The Problem

Why crypto needs a rulebook

For a decade, US crypto firms have operated in a legal gray zone. The SEC claims authority over most tokens as unregistered securities. The CFTC claims authority over Bitcoin and Ether as commodities. Exchanges don't know which agency to register with, investors don't know which protections apply, and builders routinely flee to jurisdictions with clearer rules.

The CLARITY Act is Congress's attempt to end that gray zone by drawing explicit jurisdictional lines between the SEC and the CFTC — and giving crypto exchanges a federal registration regime for the first time.

The Framework

A three-legged stool

Congress is building the crypto rulebook in three separate pieces. Think of it as a stool with three legs — each law covers a different slice of the crypto market, and all three are needed to make the framework stand on its own.

GENIUS handles the easy, bipartisan slice (dollar-backed stablecoins). CLARITY handles the middle (spot markets under CFTC authority). Senate Banking will eventually handle the contentious end (which tokens count as securities). All three must exist for US crypto to have a complete federal framework.

The Bill

What CLARITY actually does

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act (H.R. 3633) is roughly 200 pages of statute, but its core moves are simple. In plain English, it:

  • Defines "digital commodity" — gives the first clean legal definition separating commodities (BTC, ETH) from securities
  • Assigns the CFTC primary jurisdiction over digital commodity spot markets, carving them out of SEC securities law
  • Creates a federal registration regime for crypto exchanges, brokers, and dealers — the first time in US history
  • Imposes "core principles" on registered exchanges: listing standards, market surveillance, financial resources, cybersecurity, conflict-of-interest walls
  • Mandates consumer protections including customer fund segregation (the FTX problem), conflict-of-interest rules, and plain-language disclosures
  • Bars the Federal Reserve from issuing a retail central bank digital currency (CBDC)
  • Sets an 18-month rulemaking deadline — the CFTC and SEC must finish the actual rulebook within 18 months of enactment

The Complication

Why there are actually two bills

The House passed CLARITY (H.R. 3633) in July 2025 as a single bill covering both SEC and CFTC jurisdictions. But the Senate is writing its own version — and splitting it in two. This is the thing most people get wrong when they read news stories about "the crypto bill."

House · Single Bill
CLARITY Act
H.R. 3633
Covers both the CFTC side (spot markets, exchanges) and the SEC side (token classification). Passed July 2025, 294-134 bipartisan.
Senate · Split in Two
DCIA + Banking bill
S. 4064 (Ag) + Banking draft (TBD)
Senate Ag's Digital Commodity Intermediaries Act handles CFTC side only. Senate Banking writing a separate bill for the SEC side. Both must pass, then reconcile with the House.

This is the "vehicle substitution" trap. If the Senate uses its own bills instead of amending H.R. 3633, the House can't just "concur" — it either has to accept the Senate package as-is (unlikely) or send everything to a conference committee where a handful of senators and representatives negotiate a single compromise bill. Conference adds weeks to months of delay.

The Players

Who's actually driving this

House (done their job)

  • French Hill (R-AR) — Chair, House Financial Services Committee. Co-lead on CLARITY
  • GT Thompson (R-PA) — Chair, House Agriculture Committee. Co-lead on CLARITY's CFTC provisions

Senate Agriculture (done their job)

  • John Boozman (R-AR) — Chair, Senate Agriculture Committee. Sponsor of the DCIA (S. 4064)
  • Amy Klobuchar (D-MN) — Ranking Member. Worked with Boozman to pass DCIA out of committee Jan 2026

Senate Banking (the bottleneck)

  • Tim Scott (R-SC) — Chair, Senate Banking Committee. Responsible for scheduling the Banking markup (postponed January)
  • Cynthia Lummis (R-WY) — Banking subcommittee chair. Leading drafter of the Senate SEC-side text
  • Thom Tillis (R-NC) + Angela Alsobrooks (D-MD) — brokered the March 2026 stablecoin yield compromise
  • Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) — Ranking Member. Leading Democratic opposition, pushing for "gryfto" amendments targeting Trump-family conflicts of interest

The gap between August and "by 2027" — about 24 points of probability spread over 17 months — is the market's implied answer to the key question: if the bill doesn't pass before the October 2026 midterm recess, how likely is a post-election revival?

If you think the Banking markup will happen in April as scheduled, the June and July rungs look cheap.
